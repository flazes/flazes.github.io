Project 1 : Colorizing photos of the Russian Empire

CS 280A · UC Berkeley · Fall 2026

Project Overview

Sergei Mikhailovich Prokudin-Gorskii traveled the Russian Empire in the early 20th Century, and recorded the exposure of every scene onto a glass plate, using three different filters: a red, a green, a blue one. In this project, I used those negatives to retrieve colored images.

The main objective of this project is to align the color channel images and combine them to form a single RGB color image. For simple images, I divided the negative into three distinct negatives. The first one represents the blue channel, the second the green channel, and the third the red channel.

Single Scale Alignment

For simple and lower-quality images, I searched over a displacement window of $[-15,15]$ pixels in height and width. By superposing two color channels and computing a matching score, I was able to find the best match and achieve precise alignment. The score I used is the Euclidean distance (L2 score): $$ d(I_1,I_2) = \sqrt{\sum_{x,y}(I_1(x,y)-I_2(x,y))^2} $$ The smaller the distance between the two channel images, the more similar they are. Once all scores in that window are computed, the lowest score is kept. The three channels are then aligned and stacked on top of each other to produce a proper RGB image. I used np.roll along the y-axis and x-axis to displace the green and blue channels to align with the blue one, which is the one at the top of the original data. The score is not computed on the border of the images to avoid dark borders on two channels yielding a very low score.

Multi-scale Pyramid Alignment

Multi-scale image pyramid Source

The best quality data provided in .tif files were much larger, and computing the L2 distance over the same displacement window as for the previous photos would be computationally expensive. In order to save time without losing precision, I used a multi-scale pyramid alignment technique instead.

The image pyramid represents the same image at multiple scales. By starting at the smallest scaling factor, the image quality is low enough to apply the algorithm mentioned earlier over the same displacement window ($[-15,15]$).

At the end of this first rescale, the same algorithm is called recursively with the current best shift in pixels and the current scaling factor as arguments. By multiplying the scaling factor by 2 ($1/16 \rightarrow 1/8 \rightarrow 1/4 \rightarrow 1/2 \rightarrow 1$), the shift in pixels is also multiplied by 2.

This allows the algorithm to determine the region in which to search for the alignment, reducing both the size of the displacement window and the computational time. For the base case, when the scaling factor is 1 (original image size), the algorithm returns the shifted image that yields the best L2 similarity score. Then, similarly to the single-scale algorithm, the different channels are stacked using their respective colors to produce a final RGB image.

For this multi-scale alignment, I decided to align the red and blue channels on the green one, compared to aligning on the blue one for the low image quality alignment. I found that using the green channel as the reference gave better results in my experiments.

Extra Bells and Whistles

Automatic Cropping

In the original data, there are already white and black borders around the images. After alignment, these existing borders become misaligned, creating colored borders and edges where one or more color channels are missing. I implemented automatic cropping to remove these artifacts.

I wanted to detect horizontal and vertical lines near the edges of the images and assume that they corresponded to borders. To do this, I computed the average intensity of the three color channels for each row and column. I then used np.diff on these averages to find regions with the largest changes in intensity, which could indicate a horizontal or vertical border. I kept the 50 strongest candidate coordinates within the outer 20% of the image. This process was performed on each channel before alignment.

Once the alignment shift for each channel was known, I applied the same shift to the coordinates of the detected lines. This gave the corresponding line coordinates in the final aligned image. I then defined a margin of approximately 1/13 of the final image on each side and selected the detected line closest to the edge while still falling within this margin.

For example, if the left margin is at $x=230$ and the detected lines are at $x=150$, $x=210$, and $x=240$, the image would be cropped at $x=210$, since it is the closest detected border that falls within the margin.

Automatic Contrasting

As seen above, the colors of the aligned images can appear bland. Specifically, what should appear white in the images can appear a bit darker; similarly for black colors that don't appear as black as they should. To counter this, I decided to add more contrast by rescaling the lightness of the images.

To do so, I used OpenCV's library to convert the image to LAB color space. This color space has a value that represents the lightness of a pixel, ranging from 0 to 100 (0 to 255 when stored by OpenCV).

I then apply a linear interpolation to this lightness value, so that the lightness value at the 1st percentile is mapped to 0.0 and the lightness value at the 99th percentile is mapped to 255.0. Every other value is scaled accordingly. $$ L_{\mathrm{rescaled}} = \frac{L - p_1}{p_{99} - p_1} \times 255 $$ where $L$ is the original lightness value, and $p_1$ and $p_{99}$ are the 1st and 99th percentiles of the lightness values.

In the following image, the 1st percentile of lightness is 47.0 and the 99th percentile is 240.0. In the second image, after rescaling, they are 0.0 and 255.0, respectively. The dark parts of the images appear darker while staying natural.

Automatic White Balance

Despite the added automatic contrast, what should appear white may appear in different colors once aligned. This can be due to the different sources of light present at the moment the picture was taken. For example, what should appear very light gray and tend toward white might have an expected value around $(25,25,25)$. However, due to the lighting conditions, it could instead have a value such as $(23,23,30)$, which gives the image a slight blue tint.

To counter this, I estimated that the illuminant (source of light) was represented by the average color of the cropped image. I took the average value of each color channel: $\bar R$, $\bar G$, and $\bar B$. I then computed the target RGB value as the average of the three channel means: $$ T = \frac{\bar R + \bar G + \bar B}{3} $$ Finally, I corrected each channel by multiplying it by the ratio between the target value and its original channel mean: $$ R' = R \times \frac{T}{\bar R}, \qquad G' = G \times \frac{T}{\bar G}, \qquad B' = B \times \frac{T}{\bar B} $$ This brings the average value of each color channel toward the same target value, reducing the color tint caused by the lighting conditions.

Better Color Mapping

Yet after all those changes, the colors may still appear unnatural. I noticed in several pictures that the images appeared yellow-ish. Since yellow and blue appear on the opposite ends of the b* chromatic space, I introduced a small correction to the RGB channels. $$ R' = R - 0.05B $$ $$ G' = G - 0.05B $$ $$ B' = B + 0.10G $$ I wanted to reduce the yellow cast by shifting the relative balance away from yellow and toward blue, by reducing the values of red and green. I felt like increasing the values in the blue channel would also help. The small amount of green added to the blue channel was the result of several experiments, and this yielded the most natural-looking results in my experiments.

Better Features

As we have seen until here, getting the right colors is not an easy step. Yet, the current alignement techniques used so far depend on those colors, and the similarity scores between the different channels. To have a more robust alignment, I decided to change the alignement technique to use the edges on the image instead.

I first had to apply Gaussian Blur to the image in order to burry potential noise. Using OpenCV, I applied this 3x3 Gaussian blur, and then passed this blurred image to a Canny Edge Detection algorithm from OpenCV as well. This algorithm measures edge intensity for each pixel and direction. The image is then scanned to only keep local maximum in order to have singular pixels appear as edges. $$ \mathbf{G} = \sqrt{\mathbf{G}_x^2 + \mathbf{G}_y^2} $$ Here G is the intensity gradient magnitude for each pixel, which measure how much the intensity changtes around the pixel. ${G}_x$ is the derivative in the horizontal direction and ${G}_y$ the gradient in the vertical direction.

I used minVal = 100, maxVal = 200 Source

I tried several thresholds for the pixel gradients and ended up using 100 and 200 (out of 255). These thresholds determine how strong a gradient must be to be considered an edge. Gradients above 200 are kept as strong edges, while gradients below 100 are discarded. Gradients between 100 and 200 are kept depending on whether they are connected to a strong edge.

Once the edges of each channel were computed, I used a similar algorithm as before :
  • Compute the L2 distance
  • Use an image pyramid recursively to determine the number of pixels to shift the red and blue channels
  • Shift the different channels and stack them
As for the results, I eventually get the same alignement as the RGB similarity score, but I suspect this alignement method to be more robust than the previous one, due to the potential large differences in intensity between two channels. The maximum difference between the two alignement method is 2 pixels.
